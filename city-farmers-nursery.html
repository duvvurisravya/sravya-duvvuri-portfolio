5City Farmers Nursery

Rooted in nature. Growing together.

City Farmers Nursery is a family‑run garden center in City Heights, San Diego, open since 1972. It blends plant retail, education and hands‑on experiences, and quietly runs a dozen sustainability practices most customers never see. Starting from a site visit, an owner interview and a customer survey, I designed a unified brand identity and a four‑page website so the nursery feels as welcoming, and as green, online as it does in person.

Problem

A rich space, an inconsistent brand

The physical space offers a rich, community‑driven environment, but the brand doesn't carry that experience across its visual identity and digital touchpoints.

Inconsistent visual identity

The brand lacks consistency across its visual identity, so the nursery looks different from one touchpoint to the next.

Disconnected digital touchpoints

The in‑person experience isn't communicated online, which leaves a gap between the space and how people find it.

Website out of step with the store

Long‑time customers noticed the website doesn't reflect what's actually in store.

Events are hard to find

Families couldn't find clear information about workshops and events.

Research

Listening before designing

I used three methods to understand the nursery from both sides of the counter: how it actually runs, and how customers find it, shop it and come back.

Site visit

Walked the nursery as a first‑time customer would, photographing paths, signage, staff, parking and the green practices on site.

Owner interview

A semi‑structured conversation with Sam, the owner, on Sept 25, 2026: three recordings, about 34 minutes, recorded with permission.

Customer survey

A short Google Forms survey on how people shop for plants, find the nursery and use its website. Early responses span ages 18 to 65+.

Owner interview

What Sam told me

Sam's father started the nursery at 16 by rescuing plants other people threw away. Today Sam runs day‑to‑day operations and his oldest sister handles marketing and the back office.

What's working
  • Loyal, mostly 40+ customers, many of them DIY growers
  • A large email list with a strong open rate

What's hard

Sustainability in practice

Twelve green practices, almost none of them visible

The interview surfaced a long list of things the nursery already does. The design problem wasn't to make the nursery greener. It was to make what it already does visible.

Brand standards

Keeping the mark consistent

Minimum sizes

Stacked logo 1.5 in (38 mm / 60 px). Horizontal logo 2.0 in (51 mm / 80 px). Icon only 0.75 in (19 mm / 40 px).

Clear space

Keep 0.5 in of clear space around the logo so it stays balanced and easy to recognise.

Incorrect uses

Don't stretch, rotate, add effects, change colours, remove elements or swap the typeface, and avoid busy backgrounds.

Reflection

What I learned

I started this project thinking it was about a logo and a website. Talking to Sam changed that. The nursery already had a strong story, a family business that rescues plants, makes its own soil and protects the canyon next door, but almost none of it reached customers. The most useful design work was translation: turning practices people couldn't see into signs, tags, aprons and web pages they can.

It also taught me to design within real limits. No inventory system and 46 shared parking spots aren't problems a brand can fix, but a clear In Stock badge and an honest parking tip can make them easier to live with.

The brand identity began as coursework in ART 541 at San Diego State University and was extended with new research, applications and a website as my SDSU MALAS final project, Cultivating Sustainability (2026).
